Research

Two problems sit at the center of the energy transition: storing renewable electricity safely and cheaply, and producing hydrogen and chemicals with less energy and less CO2. We work on both, starting from the surface chemistry of materials and carrying it through to working cells, reactors and processes.

Electrochemical energy storage

Flow batteries that store renewable electricity safely, at any scale, for decades.

What sets flow batteries apart

Solar and wind power rise and fall with the weather, so a grid that runs on them needs storage that is safe to place anywhere, easy to scale up and built to last. Flow batteries meet these needs in ways that lithium-ion batteries cannot.

  • Safe from fire and explosion

    The electrolyte is water-based and does not burn, so there is no thermal runaway. Systems can sit next to buildings, substations and people.

  • Easy to scale

    Energy is set by the size of the tanks and power by the cell stack, so each can be expanded on its own, from a single building to grid storage of hundreds of megawatt-hours.

  • Long life

    The active materials stay dissolved and do not crack or change phase, so a system can run more than 10,000 cycles and last for decades.

  • Reusable electrolyte

    The electrolyte can be rebalanced, topped up or recovered at the end of life instead of being discarded with the battery.

The challenge

An electrolyte can dissolve only 1–2 moles of active material per litre, while solid battery materials pack about ten times more charge into the same volume. That is why a vanadium flow battery stores about 25 Wh per litre, a tenth of a lithium-ion battery, and needs large tanks. Closing this gap, while keeping the safety and scalability of a flowing liquid, is the central problem we work on.

Two ways we bring solid-level capacity into a flowing battery

Nano-electrofluid: particles that flow and react

Nano-electrofluids

Instead of dissolving the active material, we disperse it as nanoparticles of a redox-active solid. Each particle stores charge in its solid lattice and gives up or takes electrons when it touches the electrode, so the fluid can carry far more charge than a solution while still being pumped like a liquid.

The difficulty is making the fluid behave. At high loading, nanoparticles tend to clump, settle and thicken the fluid, and charge has to reach particles that touch the electrode only briefly.

What we study. Surface design rules that keep particles dispersed and the fluid pumpable at high loading, how charge moves between flowing particles and the electrode, and how these fluids perform in full flow cells.

Redox targeting: a mediator carries charge to a solid

Redox targeting (redox mediation)

Here the solid storage material never leaves the tank. A dissolved redox mediator is charged at the electrode, flows to the tank and passes its charge to the solid by a chemical reaction, then returns to be charged again. On discharge the process runs in reverse. Only a clear liquid is pumped, yet the solid in the tank sets the capacity, which can be several times that of the electrolyte alone.

Because the solid never touches the electrode, it does not need to conduct electricity and there is no slurry to clog the cell. The catch is that the mediator's potential must closely match the solid's: a mismatch of only a few tens of millivolts leaves much of the solid unused or costs extra voltage.

What we study. Pairs of mediators and solid materials that match well, and redox-mediating chemistry that raises the usable capacity of flow batteries.

Making the cell itself work better

  1. Electrode design and electrokinetics

    The reactions take place on the surface of carbon fiber electrodes. We engineer surface defects and heteroatom dopants and measure how each changes the reaction rate. Our studies show that defect sites, more than oxygen groups, control vanadium redox kinetics.

  2. Flow cell design and operando analysis

    We design flow cells and measure, while they run, how much voltage is lost at each electrode and at the membrane. Knowing where the losses occur tells us which component to improve next.

Catalysis and materials for green energy and chemicals

Catalysts and reactors for clean hydrogen and CO2 conversion.

Why it matters

Hydrogen, the chemicals made from it, and the conversion of CO2 into useful products all depend on catalysts. A better catalyst lets a reaction run at a lower temperature, with less energy and fewer by-products, which lowers both cost and emissions.

The challenge

Many of these reactions absorb or release large amounts of heat: steam reforming of methane consumes heat, and CO2 methanation releases it. Heat moves slowly through conventional ceramic catalysts, so hot and cold spots form, the catalyst deactivates and the reactor runs below its potential. Performance depends not only on the active sites but on how heat and mass reach them.

Our approach

  1. 1

    Catalysts with built-in heat transport

    We grow a thin, porous Al2O3 shell directly on aluminium metal and anchor metal nanoparticles on it. The metal core moves heat quickly while the shell provides the reaction surface, so surface chemistry and heat transport are designed together (U.S. Patent 12,734,505).

  2. 2

    Hydrogen production and CO2 conversion

    We apply these catalysts to hydrogen production from natural gas and bio-derived glycerol, and to the conversion of CO2, including electrochemical routes that turn CO2 into useful chemicals.

  3. 3

    Reaction kinetics and mechanisms

    We measure reaction rates and combine them with DFT calculations and microkinetic models to find which surface steps control a reaction. That understanding tells us how to design the next catalyst, rather than finding it by trial and error.

  4. 4

    Membrane reactors and process integration

    A hydrogen-selective membrane removes H2 as it forms, pushing the reaction toward higher conversion. We combine reaction and separation in compact units, such as a 1 kW natural gas fuel processor for residential fuel cells.

The same materials, beyond catalysis

The design principles carry over to other problems: N-doped CeO2 that removes the radicals that degrade fuel cell membranes, heat-conductive core–shell fillers and graphene for thermal interface materials in batteries and electronics, and metal–organic frameworks for gas separation.
