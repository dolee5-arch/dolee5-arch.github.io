Electrochemical energy storage

Flow batteries that store electricity safely, at any scale, for decades.

What sets flow batteries apart

Electricity demand is climbing with AI data centers and the electrification of transport and industry, while more of the supply comes from solar and wind that rise and fall with the weather. Storing electricity is becoming as important as generating it, and that storage must be safe to place anywhere, easy to scale up and built to last.

Safe from fire and explosion
Water-based electrolyte that does not burn. No thermal runaway.
Easy to scale
Tanks set the energy and the stack sets the power, so each grows on its own.
Long life
More than 10,000 cycles; the active materials do not crack or change phase.
Reusable electrolyte
Rebalanced, topped up or recovered instead of discarded.

The challenge

An electrolyte can dissolve only 1–2 moles of active material per litre, while solid battery materials pack about ten times more charge into the same volume. A vanadium flow battery therefore stores about 25 Wh per litre, a tenth of a lithium-ion battery.

Our goal is to bring the charge density of solids into a battery that still flows, without giving up its safety or scalability.

Storage materials and chemistry

Two ways to put solid-level capacity into a flowing battery

Nano-electrofluids

We disperse the active material as nanoparticles of a redox-active solid. Each particle stores charge in its solid and exchanges electrons when it touches the electrode, so the fluid carries far more charge than a solution yet is still pumped like a liquid.

What we study Surface design that keeps particles dispersed and the fluid pumpable at high loading, and how charge moves between flowing particles and the electrode.

Redox targeting

The solid stays in the tank. A dissolved mediator is charged at the electrode, flows to the tank and passes its charge to the solid, then returns. Only a clear liquid is pumped, yet the solid sets the capacity, and it does not need to conduct electricity.

What we study Mediator and solid pairs whose potentials match closely, and redox-mediating chemistry that raises usable capacity.

Electrode materials

Faster reactions at the electrode surface

Building on our work on vanadium flow batteries, we engineer defects and heteroatom dopants on carbon electrodes and measure how each changes the reaction rate. Defect sites, more than oxygen groups, control vanadium redox kinetics.

Flow cell reactors

Finding where the voltage is lost

We design flow cells and separate, while they run, the voltage lost at each electrode and at the membrane, a method we developed in vanadium flow batteries, so we know which component to improve next.

Catalyst design and kinetics

Designing catalysts from how they work

We design how a catalyst is made so that its active sites, support and heat transport are set together. Core–shell catalysts grown on aluminium metal combine a porous oxide shell carrying metal nanoparticles with a metal core that moves reaction heat (U.S. Patent 12,734,505).

Reaction rates combined with DFT calculations and microkinetic models show which surface step controls a reaction, and so which property of the catalyst to change next.

Advanced chemical reactors

Compact reactors for distributed and mobile use

Small reactors that make hydrogen where it is used must be compact, start quickly and handle frequent start–stop and changing loads, unlike a plant that runs steadily for years.

Functional materials

Materials designed for a specific function, from fuel cells to thermal management.

The control of surfaces, defects and structure that we use for catalysts and electrodes carries over to other functional materials.

  • Radical-scavenging CeO2

    N-doped CeO2 nanoparticles that remove reactive oxygen radicals and extend the life of fuel cell membranes.

  • Membranes by CVD and MOFs

    Hydrogen-selective silica membranes made by chemical vapor deposition, and stable MOF membranes for gas separation and membrane reactors.

  • Carbon electrodes and graphene

    Doped and defect-engineered carbon electrodes, and electrochemically exfoliated graphene, for batteries and electrochemical devices.

  • Thermal interface materials (TIMs)

    Heat-conductive core–shell fillers and graphene that carry heat away from batteries and electronics. Our work on these materials led to Next Materials Inc., a startup founded from the lab.
