Electrochemical energy storage

Flow batteries that store renewable electricity safely, at any scale, for decades.

  • Storage materials and chemistryNano-electrofluids, redox targeting, redox mediators
  • Electrode materialsDefect-engineered and doped carbon electrodes, electrokinetics
  • Flow cell reactorsCell design, operando analysis of voltage losses

What sets flow batteries apart

Solar and wind power rise and fall with the weather. A grid that runs on them needs storage that is safe to place anywhere, easy to scale up and built to last.

Safe from fire and explosion
Water-based electrolyte that does not burn. No thermal runaway.
Easy to scale
Tanks set the energy and the stack sets the power, so each grows on its own.
Long life
More than 10,000 cycles; the active materials do not crack or change phase.
Reusable electrolyte
Rebalanced, topped up or recovered instead of discarded.

The challenge

An electrolyte can dissolve only 1–2 moles of active material per litre, while solid battery materials pack about ten times more charge into the same volume. A vanadium flow battery therefore stores about 25 Wh per litre, a tenth of a lithium-ion battery.

Our goal is to bring the charge density of solids into a battery that still flows, without giving up its safety or scalability.

Storage materials and chemistry

Two ways to put solid-level capacity into a flowing battery

Nano-electrofluids

We disperse the active material as nanoparticles of a redox-active solid. Each particle stores charge in its solid and exchanges electrons when it touches the electrode, so the fluid carries far more charge than a solution yet is still pumped like a liquid.

What we study Surface design that keeps particles dispersed and the fluid pumpable at high loading, and how charge moves between flowing particles and the electrode.

Redox targeting

The solid stays in the tank. A dissolved mediator is charged at the electrode, flows to the tank and passes its charge to the solid, then returns. Only a clear liquid is pumped, yet the solid sets the capacity, and it does not need to conduct electricity.

What we study Mediator and solid pairs whose potentials match closely, and redox-mediating chemistry that raises usable capacity.

Electrode materials

Faster reactions at the electrode surface

We engineer defects and heteroatom dopants on carbon electrodes and measure how each changes the reaction rate. Defect sites, more than oxygen groups, control vanadium redox kinetics.

Flow cell reactors

Finding where the voltage is lost

We design flow cells and separate, while they run, the voltage lost at each electrode and at the membrane, so we know which component to improve next.

Catalysis and materials for green energy and chemicals

Catalysts and reactors for clean hydrogen and CO2 conversion.

Why it matters

Hydrogen, the chemicals made from it, and the conversion of CO2 into useful products all depend on catalysts. A better catalyst lets a reaction run at a lower temperature, with less energy and fewer by-products, which lowers both cost and emissions.

The challenge

Many of these reactions absorb or release large amounts of heat: steam reforming of methane consumes heat, and CO2 methanation releases it. Heat moves slowly through conventional ceramic catalysts, so hot and cold spots form, the catalyst deactivates and the reactor runs below its potential. Performance depends not only on the active sites but on how heat and mass reach them.

Our approach

  1. 1

    Catalysts with built-in heat transport

    We grow a thin, porous Al2O3 shell directly on aluminium metal and anchor metal nanoparticles on it. The metal core moves heat quickly while the shell provides the reaction surface, so surface chemistry and heat transport are designed together (U.S. Patent 12,734,505).

  2. 2

    Hydrogen production and CO2 conversion

    We apply these catalysts to hydrogen production from natural gas and bio-derived glycerol, and to the conversion of CO2, including electrochemical routes that turn CO2 into useful chemicals.

  3. 3

    Reaction kinetics and mechanisms

    We measure reaction rates and combine them with DFT calculations and microkinetic models to find which surface steps control a reaction. That understanding tells us how to design the next catalyst, rather than finding it by trial and error.

  4. 4

    Membrane reactors and process integration

    A hydrogen-selective membrane removes H2 as it forms, pushing the reaction toward higher conversion. We combine reaction and separation in compact units, such as a 1 kW natural gas fuel processor for residential fuel cells.

The same materials, beyond catalysis

The design principles carry over to other problems: N-doped CeO2 that removes the radicals that degrade fuel cell membranes, heat-conductive core–shell fillers and graphene for thermal interface materials in batteries and electronics, and metal–organic frameworks for gas separation.
